What is MSX?

One standard for home computers, built by dozens of companies, so a cartridge or a tape bought for one brand ran on all of them. It ruled Japan, Korea and Brazil in the 1980s, and it is the machine Ok-Prompt emulates.

1One standard, many makers

MSX was conceived by Microsoft for the Japanese market and pushed by Kazuhiko Nishi of ASCII Corporation. It was announced in June 1983 (sources give both the 16th and the 27th), and the first machine, the Mitsubishi ML-8000, went on sale on 21 October 1983.

The idea was the one VHS had brought to video: a single specification that Sony, Panasonic, Yamaha, Philips, Toshiba, Canon, Sanyo and many others would all build to. A cartridge works in any MSX; a tape saved on one loads on another. Microsoft called it "an attempt to create unified standards" for home computers.

What does MSX stand for?

Nobody agrees, including its creator:

  • Microsoft eXtended, after MSX BASIC: the popular belief.
  • Matsushita–Sony, the two giants behind it.
  • Machines with Software eXchangeability: Nishi, in 2001.
  • The MX missile: Nishi again, in 1985.
  • "The next of Microsoft", with Matsushita and Sony's initials inside: Nishi's 2020 book.

3Around the world

MSX was the leading home computer of the 1980s in Japan, South Korea, Argentina and Brazil, and fairly popular in the Netherlands and Spain. Yamaha sold whole networked classrooms (teacher machine plus student machines) to schools in the USSR, Cuba and Arab countries.

It never took off in the US, where only Spectravideo and Yamaha tried briefly, or the UK, where it arrived late (September 1984) and dearer than the Spectrum, Commodore 64 and Amstrad.

How many were sold is disputed: from about 4 million (Nishi) to 9 million (other estimates).

  • Sony HitBit HB-75 (April 1984, ¥69,800) and a long line up to the MSX2+ HB-F1XV (1989).
  • Yamaha CX5M (1984): an MSX with a built-in FM synthesizer (YM2151) and MIDI out, for the DX7 era.
  • Philips VG-8020 (1984), built by Kyocera; versions for each European country.
  • Spectravideo SVI-728: the US company whose SV-328 was almost an MSX before MSX.
  • Daewoo IQ-1000 and the Zemmix consoles in Korea; Samsung's SPC-800.
  • Sakhr AX150: made by Yamaha for Kuwait's Al Alamiah, with Arabic, for Egypt and the Gulf.
  • Panasonic, National, Toshiba, Canon, Sanyo, Mitsubishi and more in Japan.

4Brazil: Expert and Hotbit

On 29 October 1984 Brazil passed Lei 7.232, the Política Nacional de Informática: the market for micro and minicomputers was reserved for companies with Brazilian capital (the reserva de mercado), overseen by the SEI. Foreign machines could not simply be imported; computers were made at home, often as local versions of foreign designs. The reserve lasted until the early 1990s (sources say 1991 or 1992).

MSX arrived that way, twice in one week. The Sharp Hotbit HB-8000, made by Sharp's Brazilian subsidiary through its Epcom division in the Manaus free zone, came out in November 1985: the first Brazilian MSX. The Gradiente Expert XP-800, shown at the Informática '85 fair in São Paulo, followed on 1 December 1985 at Cr$ 4.640.000 (about US$470).

Between them, MSX "virtually killed" the other 8-bit computers in Brazil. Magazines such as Micro Sistemas, CPU MSX and MSX Micro printed listings and reviews, and kits from companies such as DDX later turned Experts and Hotbits into MSX2 and MSX2+ machines. The community never left: Clube MSX still prints a magazine, and MSXRio meetings ran for years.

Gradiente Expert

A home-stereo look with a separate keyboard (QWERTY with ç, a numeric pad, GRAPH and CODE labelled LGRA and RGRA) on its own connector. No CAPS lamp, no reset key. Often called a copy of Panasonic's National CF-3000; msx.org notes Gradiente was not an official MSX licensee.

  • XP-800 (1985): BASIC 1.0
  • GPC-1 (1987): BASIC 1.1, TV adapter built in
  • Expert Plus (1989): black case
  • Expert DD Plus (1989): 3½" 720 KB drive, reset button

Gradiente never made an MSX2 and left MSX in 1990.

Sharp Hotbit HB-8000

One-piece case with a QWERTY keyboard with ç, 64 KB of RAM, PAL-M TV output, a Centronics printer port and optional 5¼" drives. msx.org says it "followed MSX standards strictly".

  • 1.0 and 1.1 (1985): white and grey; no 1.0 ROM has ever been dumped
  • 1.2 (1987): black case

Production ended in 1988.

Brazil used PAL-M: PAL colour on a 525-line, 60 Hz picture, like NTSC. So Brazilian MSXs are 60 Hz machines, with game music at Japanese speed rather than the slower European 50 Hz.

Why 1.0 and 1.1 did not get along

The 1.0 put the Portuguese letters wherever it could: ç and Ç on two ASCII codes, the tilde letters on the umlaut codes. Text written on a 1.0 came out as ` and ä elsewhere, and programs printing ` or ~ showed ç on a 1.0. Gradiente recalled most XP-800s, swapping the ROM for 1.1 and changing nine keys, which is why most "Expert 1.0" machines today really have 1.1. Version 1.1 moved the letters to the codes that became BRASCII, which ended the accent incompatibilities between makers.

Comparing the two ROMs in Ok-Prompt: 580 bytes differ, in 84 places, most of them in the keyboard routines and the key table (20 of its 288 entries).

The Expert 1.0 credits

On a 1.0, type:

DEFUSR=5032:A=USR(0)

Other differences the sources record

  • Expert hardware: the numeric pad sits at non-standard places in the keyboard matrix; the printer port is Centronics on a non-standard 26-pin IDC socket; the RGB connector carries +12 V on one pin; the expansion bus shares slot 3 with cartridge slot B.
  • Expert Plus and DD Plus use a Toshiba MSX-Engine (T7937A) whose video chip lacks some undocumented TMS9918 tricks, and moved the RAM to slot 3-0. Game loaders written for the Expert 1.1 and Hotbit looked for it in the wrong place and crashed.
  • Hotbit 1.2: its PLAY command counts too few ticks for a 60 Hz machine, so music plays too fast.
  • Which ROM fixed what: sources disagree on whether Hotbit 1.1 or 1.2 fixed the character table to match the Expert.
  • The BIOS ID bytes at 002Bh–002Ch say the character set, date format, 50/60 Hz and keyboard. No value was ever defined for Brazil: both Brazilian makers say "international".

6MSX2, MSX2+ and turbo R

Each generation kept the last one's software running: an MSX1 cartridge, tape or disk works on an MSX2, an MSX2+ and a turbo R. What changed was mostly the picture, then the speed.

The machines

Europe's MSX2s were mostly Dutch and Spanish: the Philips NMS 8250 (1986, 128K, a 720K drive, made by Sanyo), the 8255 with two drives and the 8280 (1987), and Sony's HB-F700P, made for Europe only. Japan had the cheap Panasonic FS-A1 (1986, ¥29,800) and Sony's HB-F1 line.

The MSX2+ came only from Panasonic (FS-A1WX, WSX, FX), Sony (HB-F1XDJ, and the HB-F1XV of 1989, its last MSX) and Sanyo (the WAVY PHC-70FD). ASCII, which developed the standard alone after Microsoft left in 1986, lost interest in Europe, and not one MSX2+ was launched there.

The turbo R was meant to be the MSX3, but Yamaha's new video chip was not ready. ASCII designed a CPU instead, the R800: twice the Z80's clock, a 16-bit ALU, most instructions in one to a few cycles. Only Panasonic built it: the FS-A1ST (1990, ¥87,800) and the FS-A1GT (December 1991, ¥99,800), the last MSX ever made.

MSX2 and 2+ in Brazil

No MSX2 was ever made in Brazil, though Gradiente's users waited for one. Instead, Brazilians upgraded: kits by ACVS (Ademir Carchano, later CIEL) and DDX (Digital Design) turned an Expert or a Hotbit into an MSX2 or MSX2+, with the new video chip, a memory mapper, the SUB-ROM and, on the Kit 2+, FM sound. The msx.org wiki notes that MSX2+ upgrades were sold mainly in Brazil. Carchano later made whole MSX2+ boards for the Expert case (CIEL Expert 2+, 3 and 4 Turbo).

Ok-Prompt boots the 2+ kit's ROMs for the Expert and the Hotbit, FM-BASIC included measured.

How an MSX2+ knows it was reset

Port F4h keeps a bit through a reset: the BIOS sets it once started, and on a warm boot skips its logo and leaves RAM alone. Without it, the kits' logo never showed measured.

7Disk drives

Tapes were slow and fiddly; a disk drive turned an MSX into a small office computer, with MSX-DOS running CP/M programs (see the next chapter). How it worked is a neat piece of design.

From cartridge to built in

On an MSX1 the drive came as a cartridge interface with a cable to the drive: the Philips VY-0010 and Sony HBD-50 (both 1985) for single-sided 3.5" disks (360K), the Sony HBD-F1 for double-sided ones (720K). On the MSX2 the interface was still optional, but many machines had one or two 3.5" drives built in, and software came on 360K and 720K disks.

The disk ROM

Every interface carries a 16K disk ROM holding Disk BASIC and the MSX-DOS kernel, the same for every maker, plus a small driver for its own controller. The kernel only ever calls the driver through a fixed jump table: 4010h DSKIO reads or writes sectors, 4013h DSKCHG asks if the disk was changed, 4016h GETDPB describes its format, 401Ch DSKFMT formats it. So a maker wrote only the driver, and any disk ROM works in any MSX.

At start-up the BIOS finds the ROM in its slot and lets it set up its work area in RAM, which is why BASIC shows fewer bytes free with a drive. Then it reads the disk's first sector and runs it: a disk with MSXDOS.SYS starts MSX-DOS, any other leaves you in Disk BASIC. An MSX1 has no clock, so the date starts at 1 January 1984 unless you give one.

The controller

Many interfaces use a Western Digital WD2793 floppy controller (Philips, Sony, and the Brazilian Microsol and DDX); Panasonic used a Toshiba TC8566AF. Almost all put the controller's registers in memory, inside the ROM's slot (Philips at 7FF8h, Panasonic at 7FB8h). The Brazilian Microsol CDX-2 and DDX used I/O ports instead, D0h–D4h, which is why their disk ROMs are their own.

The DOSes

  • MSX-DOS 1 (Microsoft, 1984, by Tim Paterson): FAT12 disks a PC can read, CP/M programs, no subdirectories.
  • MSX-DOS 2 (ASCII, July 1988): subdirectories, the memory mapper, pipes and redirection; a cartridge, then built into the turbo R.
  • SOLX-DOS (Microsol, 1986): a Brazilian DOS in Portuguese for the CDX-2, kept up to 2005.
  • Nextor (Konamiman, 2011): MSX-DOS 2.31 carried on, with FAT16 and hard disks and SD cards.

FAST!DiskROM

In 2005 Adriano Camargo Rodrigues da Cunha (A&L Software) wrote a new disk ROM for the CDX-2 and the DDX, Liftron and DMX interfaces: about 20 KB/s against about 5 for the originals, MSX-DOS 2 compatible, up to four drives, more memory left for BASIC.

In Ok-Prompt, any plain disk ROM works: the machine answers the jump table itself on a .dsk image, whatever controller the ROM was written for. Tested with the Philips VG-8235, Sony HB-3600, Microsol CDX-2 and FAST!DiskROM ROMs on the Expert, the Hotbit and the 2+ kit; on an Expert 1.1, BASIC keeps 23,432 bytes with the Philips ROM and 25,429 with FAST!DiskROM measured.

8MSX and CP/M

Ok-Prompt's sibling, A-Prompt, emulates CP/M on the same Z80 core. The two standards attacked the same problem from opposite ends, and met in MSX-DOS.

CP/M (Gary Kildall, 1974) made software portable across hardware that differed: each maker wrote a small BIOS, and every program talked to the BDOS through CALL 5. By 1982 it was licensed for more than 450 kinds of machine.

MSX made the hardware the same, so programs could even go straight to the chips. But it needed a disk system too. In August 1983 Microsoft hired Tim Paterson, the author of 86-DOS (which became MS-DOS), to write a "Z80 MS-DOS". He reasoned it should run CP/M programs directly. MSX-DOS 1.00 is dated 20 June 1984.

The result is a hybrid: MS-DOS outside, CP/M inside. Programs load at 0100h and call 0005h like CP/M programs; the FCB calls are CP/M 2.2's; WordStar, Turbo Pascal and Microsoft's M80 and L80 were, in principle, compatible. But disks are FAT12, readable on a PC, and the command line is a COMMAND.COM, not a CCP. MSX-DOS 2 (1988) added subdirectories, and Nextor carries it on today.

9Games and legacy

Konami made the MSX a games machine: Knightmare (1986), Gradius (called Nemesis in Europe, 1986), Nemesis 2 (1987). From 1987 Konami's cartridges carried the SCC, a sound chip with five channels of 32-byte waveforms, conceived with Toshiba in 1986; fifteen games used it. Ok-Prompt plays it.

Hideo Kojima's first game, Metal Gear, came out on the MSX2 on 13 July 1987, and the first two Metal Gear games are MSX games. In Brazil, software houses sold programs in Portuguese: one 1987 Micro Sistemas ad lists more than 150 titles for MSX and the TK-90X. Ok-Prompt loads .cas tapes and types BLOAD"CAS:",R for you.

10The PC took it all

MSX was meant to be the VHS of home computers: one standard, every brand. It ended as a lost art, and the machine that won was the one nobody owned.

Japan: the parents walk away

In early 1986 Microsoft ended its partnership with ASCII, saying Nishi owed it more than $500,000, and set up its own Japanese company. ASCII carried MSX alone, while Microsoft's future was MS-DOS and Windows. In Japan, as the story is usually told, games went to Nintendo's Famicom and serious computing to NEC's PC-98. Sony stopped with the HB-F1XV in 1989; Panasonic made the last MSX, the FS-A1GT, in 1991, and stopped production a couple of years later (sources say 1993 to 1995), saying people had lost interest in home computers, not that sales had dropped.

Europe: one maker too few

In Europe MSX had fought the Commodore 64, the Spectrum and the Amstrad, then the Amiga and the Atari ST. Philips, its main backer there, made its last MSX2s in 1987, and no MSX2+ ever crossed over.

Brazil: the market opens

Brazil's MSXs existed because of the market reserve: from October 1984 the law kept microcomputers for national companies. Sharp's Epcom stopped the Hotbit in 1988, and Gradiente left MSX in 1990. In October 1991 a new law replaced the reserve with tax incentives, the old one expired in October 1992, and imported parts and PC clones came in. About 400,000 MSXs had been made in Brazil between 1985 and 1992.

Why the PC won

An MSX needed a licence: ASCII and Microsoft decided what the standard was, and only they could move it forward. The IBM PC became open by accident. In 1983 and 1984 Compaq and then Phoenix rebuilt its BIOS by clean-room design, legally, and Microsoft kept the right to sell MS-DOS to anyone. From then on any factory, in Taiwan, in Manaus, anywhere, could build a PC without asking, and each one made the next one cheaper.

That last point is our reading of the history, not a quotation; the clean-room BIOS facts are Wikipedia's.

What survived

  • The MSX Association, led by Kazuhiko Nishi, holds the rights. It backed the FPGA 1chipMSX (2006) and the ESP32-based MSX0 (shipping from 2023), and announced an MSX3 in 2022, whose future is unclear.
  • The games came back: on the Japanese Wii Virtual Console (2007–2009), Metal Gear among them, and on Project EGG.
  • Emulators: openMSX (2002), blueMSX (2003), and WebMSX by the Brazilian Paulo Peccin. Ok-Prompt joins them.
  • Brazil kept the flame: the MSXRio meetings since 1997 (the 45th in April 2024), the MSXBR-L mailing list, and the sites that kept the ROMs and the schematics.
